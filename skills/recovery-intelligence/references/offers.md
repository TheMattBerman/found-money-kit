# Offers

Governs `tables/offer-direction.json`.

## Direction depends on relationship

The single most useful distinction in the corpus, and it keys on the same
prospect/customer split the valuation rule already uses.

**Prospect who said no → step down.** They are telling you the price, scope, or
commitment is a mismatch. Reduce scope to meet them: a payment plan, a lower
feature tier, or a trial with a catch. This is not a discount on the core product.

**Customer who bought and left → step up.** They already trust you and drifted.
Hold price and add value or a commitment structure: a cookie, a continuity bonus,
waived-fee continuity, or non-monetary terms.

**Involuntary lapse → repair, not offer.** Assume a technical fault. Offer no
incentive at all unless they tell you money is tight, and then payment-plan
flexibility only.

## The anchored ladder

Rungs are meaningful only relative to each other, which is why three independent
variants is the wrong shape. The clearest corpus example is waived-fee continuity,
presented as a choice:

> Month-to-month flexibility at the premium rate, **or** a waived setup fee in
> exchange for a committed term. Break the commitment early and you pay back the
> waived value.

The anchor exists to make the core choosable. The downsell preserves the same
recovery outcome with less scope, support, or commitment. It is a fallback, not a
discounted clone.

Every rung carries a condition: a milestone, a share of results, an implementation
step, or a clawback. A price and a name are not an offer.

## Guardrails, and why they are enforced rather than advised

**A discount must buy a commitment.** Discount continuity works: a real price
break against an upfront annual commitment. An unbound percentage off a
month-to-month price is a panic discount that erodes the margin that funds
acquisition.

The corpus names the exact failure mode of an automated win-back product:

> "If your system automatically triggers a 20% discount email 30 days after a
> customer goes quiet, you actively train your highest-value customers to stop
> paying full price and systematically wait out your automated win-back sequence."

Found Money is that system unless the rule is enforced rather than suggested.

**Unpriced piles concede nothing.** When you do not know what someone is worth,
never spend cash or give a price concession on the first touchpoint. Lead with
zero-cost generosity instead — an asset that costs nothing to replicate. On real
data most piles are unpriced, so this is the common case, not the edge case.

**Give up terms, not price.** Turnaround time, contract duration, payment
schedule, access. Emotional compensation is reported as worth several times dollar
compensation, and price is the lazy lever.

## Honest urgency

Scarcity must be structural: real capacity limits, a real inventory bound, a real
start date. "The train leaves at 1:00 PM" — you are pointing at a moving train,
not pretending a door is closing.

Never fabricate a date. If you say it expires Tuesday, it expires Tuesday.
